**Fundamentals** shows how healthy and how expensive a company is, using its financial statements. Where the chart shows what the price has done, this screen shows what the **business** has done. Open it with the **bank** icon in the chart header, between Analyst Reports and Option Stats.

![The Fundamentals screen](fundamentals-screen.png)

The screen has four sections: **Valuation**, **Profitability**, **Balance sheet** and **Annual results**. A dash (—) means the data provider has no figure for that item. Some figures, such as P/E, have no meaning for a company that is losing money.

> **TTM** means *trailing twelve months*: the most recent four quarters added together, so the figure stays up to date between annual reports.

## Valuation

How much investors are paying for the company.

| Figure | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Market cap** | The total value of all the shares: share price times number of shares. Shown in a short form, so *2.85T* is 2.85 trillion dollars |
| **P/E (TTM)** | Price to earnings: the share price divided by the profit per share. A P/E of 30 means investors pay $30 for each $1 of yearly profit |
| **P/S** | Price to sales: market cap divided by revenue. Useful for companies with little or no profit |
| **P/B** | Price to book: the price compared with the company's net assets on the balance sheet |
| **EV/EBITDA** | Enterprise value (market cap plus debt, less cash) divided by operating profit before some costs. It lets you compare companies with different amounts of debt |
| **PEG** | The P/E divided by the expected growth rate of earnings. Around 1 suggests the price is in line with growth, and higher suggests you are paying more for each point of growth |

**Reading it.** A high number means the stock is expensive compared with what the company earns, sells or owns. That is not automatically bad, because fast-growing companies often deserve higher ratios, but it means more has to go right to justify the price. Compare a company with **others in its industry**, since normal levels differ widely: technology firms usually have higher P/E ratios than banks or utilities.

## Profitability

How much profit the company keeps from each dollar of sales, and how well it uses money.

| Figure | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Gross margin** | Revenue left after the direct cost of making the product. Higher means more room to cover other costs |
| **Operating margin** | What is left after running the business (staff, marketing, research) |
| **Net margin** | The share of revenue that ends up as final profit after everything, including interest and tax |
| **ROE** | Return on equity: yearly profit divided by shareholders' money. It shows how well the company uses what owners have put in |
| **ROA** | Return on assets: yearly profit divided by everything the company owns |

All five are shown as percentages. A 25.7% net margin means the company keeps about 26 cents of every dollar of revenue as profit. Stable or rising margins are a good sign, and falling margins can warn of rising costs or price pressure.

**A caution on ROE.** Companies that borrow a lot, or that have bought back many of their own shares, can show a very high ROE even when the business is ordinary, because the equity in the formula is small. Check ROE alongside debt.

## Balance sheet

What the company owns and owes.

| Figure | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Cash** | Cash and short-term holdings it can use right away |
| **Total debt** | Everything it has borrowed |
| **Debt / equity** | Debt divided by shareholders' equity. Higher means more reliance on borrowed money |
| **Current ratio** | Short-term assets divided by short-term bills. Above 1 means it can cover what it owes in the next year, and below 1 means it depends on new money coming in |
| **Total equity** | What would be left for shareholders after paying all debts |

These come from the company's **latest annual balance sheet**, apart from the debt-to-equity and current ratios, which are trailing figures. A company with plenty of cash and little debt can ride out a bad year, and one with large debts and little cash has less room for mistakes. Many strong companies run a current ratio below 1 on purpose, so use it as a prompt to look closer, not as a verdict.

## Annual results

A table of the company's **last four fiscal years**, newest first.

| Column | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Year** | The fiscal year (a company's financial year may not match the calendar year) |
| **Revenue** | Total sales |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue minus the direct cost of goods sold |
| **Operating income** | Profit from running the business, before interest and tax |
| **Net income** | Final profit after everything |
| **EPS** | Earnings per share (diluted): net income divided by the number of shares |
| **Free cash flow** | Cash left after running the business and paying for equipment and buildings. It is the money available for dividends, buybacks, debt repayment or growth |

Read across a row to see how revenue turns into profit, and down a column to see the trend. Look for **growth in revenue and EPS**, **profit that is converted into cash** (free cash flow near net income), and no sudden drops. Numbers use a short form, so *408B* is 408 billion dollars.

## What this screen does not show

- It shows **annual** figures only. Quarterly results and the dates of earnings reports are not here.
- It does not compare the company with its **competitors or its industry average**. Look those figures up or ask Claude.
- The figures come from the data provider and are only as current as the company's latest filing.
- Financial figures say how the business has done. They do not say where the price will go next.

## Putting fundamentals to use

- **Start with the business.** Is revenue growing? Is it profitable? Does it have more cash than debt?
- **Then look at the price.** Compare P/E, P/S and EV/EBITDA with the company's own history and with similar companies.
- **Combine with the forecasts.** Analyst estimates show where growth is expected to go. See *Analyst reports*.
- **Combine with the chart.** A strong business in a downtrend is not a signal to buy on its own. See *The chart screen*.
- **Remember the journal.** Write down why a company's numbers made you take a trade, so you can judge that reasoning later. See *Trading journal*.

## Asking Claude

Claude can read these figures. Ask *Is AAPL expensive compared with its growth?*, *Explain these margins in simple terms* or *What would worry you about this balance sheet?* and it will explain using the real numbers, while reminding you that fundamentals alone do not time a trade.
